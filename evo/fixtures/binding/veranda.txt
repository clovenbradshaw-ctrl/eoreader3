The Inheritance

The lawyer arrived on the afternoon train, and Harriet met him on the veranda where the light was still good. She had dressed carefully, though she told herself it did not matter, and she poured the tea before he had quite settled into the chair.

"Your uncle's estate is smaller than the family believes," Mr. Calloway said, opening the folder across his knees. "There are debts no one spoke of."

Harriet had expected as much, and she found that the confirmation steadied rather than frightened her. "How much is left, when the debts are paid?"

"The house, and a little besides." He watched her over his spectacles, as though the figure were a kind of test. "Most women in your position would sell the house."

She set down the pot and considered the long lawn running down to the river, where her uncle had taught her to fish before the quarrel that none of them ever explained. "I am not most women," she said, "and I am not going to sell my uncle's house to pay for his secrets."

Calloway almost smiled. He had handled the family's affairs for thirty years, and he had not expected to be surprised this late in the business. "Then we shall have to find another way," he said, and he drew a fresh sheet of paper toward him.
